Setting

Election audits: when and why

  • An election reports an outcome. A risk-limiting audit checks it against the paper ballots (Stark 2008; Lindeman and Stark 2012).
  • If the reported outcome is wrong, the audit escalates to a full hand count with probability at least \(1-\alpha\).
  • If it is right, we would like to confirm that from as few ballots as possible.
  • Used in practice: TODO add the jurisdictions you want to name (US states, pilot audits), with sources.

Timing: 1 minute. The when: after the count, before certification. The why: paper ballots are the ground truth, and the audit is the only step that looks at them statistically.

Ballot polling is a sequential test

  • Draw ballots uniformly at random, one at a time: \(X_1, X_2, \dots\)
  • After each draw, either stop and certify, or keep sampling.
  • \(H_0\): the reported winner did not win. Certifying is rejecting \(H_0\).
  • Risk-limiting means level \(\alpha\) under the audit’s stopping rule, whatever it is: \[\sup_{\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta_0} P_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\text{certify}) \le \alpha.\]
  • Throughout: sampling with replacement, so the \(X_t\) are i.i.d.

Timing: 1 minute. Flag with-replacement early; the backup slide has the finite-population argument if asked.

Two candidates: a Bernoulli test

  • \(\theta\) is the reported winner’s share, \(X_t \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\theta)\).
  • \(H_0: \theta \le 1/2\) against \(H_1: \theta > 1/2\).
  • BRAVO (Lindeman et al. 2012) and ALPHA (Stark 2023) solve this well.

Timing: 1 minute. Keep this short. The point to plant is that the null is an interval with one boundary point, the tie.

Three candidates: the plurality null

  • \(\Theta_1 = \{\theta_1 > \theta_2 \text{ and } \theta_1 > \theta_3\}\)
  • \(\Theta_0 = \{\theta_1 \le \theta_2\} \cup \{\theta_1 \le \theta_3\}\)
  • A union of two half-spaces, and not convex.
  • Its convex hull is \(\{\theta_1 \le 1/2\}\).

Timing: 1 minute. The non-convexity is the source of every difficulty that follows; say it in those words. The gap between the null and its hull is where candidate 1 has a plurality but not a majority.

The question

The state of the art runs one test per pair

  • SHANGRLA (Stark 2020): test \(H_{02}: \theta_1 \le \theta_2\) and \(H_{03}: \theta_1 \le \theta_3\) separately, each at level \(\alpha\).
  • Certify when every pairwise null is rejected: an intersection-union test (Berger 1982).

Timing: 1 minute. Valid and convenient, since each pair is a one-dimensional problem we already know how to solve.

Why not just do one test?

Two tests are valid.
Is one test on the original null better?

  • At a fixed sample size, intersection-union tests can be inadmissible (Berger et al. 1994).
  • For sequential tests, measured in ballots sampled, nobody knows.

Timing: 1 minute. This is the thesis question. Say it as “what does the decomposition cost, in ballots?”

Why it is hard

Bayesian tests handle the null directly

  • Put a prior \(W_1\) on \(\Theta_1\) and \(W_0\) on \(\Theta_0\).
  • Bayes factor \(B_t = p_{W_1}(X^t) / p_{W_0}(X^t)\) needs no decomposition.
  • Bayesian audits do this (Rivest and Shen 2012; Huang et al. 2020).

Timing: 1 minute. The appeal is that the prior lives on the whole simplex, so the non-convex null is no obstacle to writing the test down.

But Type I error control matters

  • \(B_t\) is a martingale under the prior mixture \(P_{W_0}\), not under each \(P_{\boldsymbol\theta}\), \(\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta_0\).
  • So “reject when \(B_t > 1/\alpha\)” is not risk-limiting in general.
  • A likelihood ratio test does not just work either. More on that later.

Timing: 1 minute. Say the last line exactly as written. It is repeated verbatim when the loop closes in the higher-dimensions section.

Controlling Type I error with a maximum sample size

  • Fix a maximum sample size \(T\). Then \(\bar B = \sup_{t \le T} B_t\) is bounded.
  • Threshold: the worst-case \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of \(\bar B\) over \(\Theta_0\).
  • Symmetric Dirichlet priors: the worst case is on the boundary, and Monte Carlo puts it at the two-way tie.

E-variables and martingales

E-variables: bets against the null

  • An e-variable is \(E \ge 0\) with \(\mathbb{E}_P[E] \le 1\) for every \(P\) in the null (Vovk and Wang 2021; Ramdas et al. 2023).
  • Reject when \(E \ge 1/\alpha\). By Markov’s inequality this is level \(\alpha\).
  • Betting reading (Shafer 2021): \(E\) is the payoff of a bet that costs 1. The house accepts only bets that cannot win on average under the null.
  • Evidence is money won.

Timing: 1 minute. The gambler believes Q, the house believes the null. Keep this picture; log-optimality is the gambler’s strategy.

Optional stopping and optional continuation

  • A test supermartingale \(S_t\): \(S_0 = 1\), nonnegative, a supermartingale under every null distribution.
  • Ville’s inequality (Ville 1939): \(P(\sup_t S_t \ge 1/\alpha) \le \alpha\).
  • So stop whenever you like, and stopped \(S_\tau\) is still an e-variable.
  • Continue whenever you like: reinvest the winnings, and products of e-variables from successive batches stay valid.

Timing: 1 minute. Optional continuation is what the batch construction at the end relies on, so say it here.

Two candidates: the likelihood ratio

\[E = \prod_{t=1}^n \left(\frac{\mu}{\nu}\right)^{X_t}\left(\frac{1-\mu}{1-\nu}\right)^{1-X_t}, \qquad g(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[E] = \left(\theta\frac{\mu}{\nu} + (1-\theta)\frac{1-\mu}{1-\nu}\right)^n.\]

Timing: 2 minutes, including the demo. The curve always crosses 1 at theta = nu. That is the fact that fails in two dimensions.

Demo: drag the blue triangle (mu) and the vermillion circle (nu) along the axis. nu snaps to 1/2.

  1. Start at nu = 1/2: the curve crosses 1 exactly at the tie, valid.
  2. Drag nu to 0.4: the curve crosses 1 at 0.4, and the band (0.4, 0.5] goes above 1 (thick stroke).
  3. Drag nu to 0.55: valid again, but the growth readout drops.
  4. Move the n slider: the curve steepens but still crosses 1 at nu. This is monotone likelihood ratio at work, and why one null point serves every n.

Log-optimality

  • Many e-variables are valid. Which is best?
  • Growth \(\mathbb{E}_Q[\log E]\): a gambler stopping at \(1/\alpha\) needs about \(\log(1/\alpha) / \mathbb{E}_Q[\log E]\) rounds (Breiman 1961; Kelly 1956).
  • For a point alternative \(\mu > 1/2\), the likelihood ratio against the tie is log-optimal.
  • For a mixture \(Q = P_{W_1}\) over the alternative, \(p_{W_1}/p_{1/2}\) is log-optimal for \(Q\).
  • The tie is already a mixture over the null. It just has one atom.

Timing: 1 minute. The last bullet is the setup for the resolution later: in higher dimensions the optimal denominator is a genuine mixture. It also explains last year’s result: the worst case of the Bayesian audit was the two-way tie for the same reason.

Martingales with an estimator

  • Replace the fixed \(\mu\) by a predictable estimate \(\hat\mu_t\) from \(X_1,\dots,X_{t-1}\): \[S_t = S_{t-1}\left(\frac{\hat\mu_t}{1/2}X_t + \frac{1-\hat\mu_t}{1/2}(1-X_t)\right).\]
  • Still a test supermartingale for \(\theta \le 1/2\), whatever the estimates.
  • This is ALPHA (Stark 2023), the method used in practice.

Timing: 45 seconds. No need to specify ALPHA’s truncated shrinkage estimator.

Back to plurality: one martingale per pair

  • One ALPHA martingale \(S^{(j)}_t\) per pair, ignoring ballots for neither candidate.
  • Certify once every pair has crossed \(1/\alpha\).

Timing: 45 seconds. The figure shows each pair as a projection to a one-dimensional problem with the tie as its null point.

It is a minimum

  • Certifying when every pair has rejected means thresholding \(\min_j E_j\).
  • \(\min_j E_j\) is an e-variable for \(\Theta_0 = \bigcup_j \Theta_{0j}\): under \(\Theta_{0j}\) it is at most \(E_j\).
  • The log-optimal e-variable is a likelihood ratio \(dQ/dP^*\).
  • Surely the optimum does not contain a min?

Timing: 45 seconds. Leave it as a question. The answer we can give at a fixed sample size is on the conjecture slide at the end.

So work in the original dimension

  • Build one e-variable for \(\Theta_0\) directly, in \(K - 1\) dimensions.
  • Then compare it with the minimum of the pairwise ones.

Higher dimensions

A likelihood ratio does not just work

  • More on that later: this is later.
  • One null point \(\boldsymbol\nu\) against the alternative \(\boldsymbol\mu\): \[g_{\boldsymbol\nu}(\boldsymbol\theta) = \left(\sum_i \theta_i \frac{\mu_i}{\nu_i}\right)^n.\]
  • \(g = 1\) is a straight line through \(\boldsymbol\nu\). A non-convex null is never on one side of it.

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Repeat the teaser sentence from “Why it is hard” word for word when this slide opens. Monotone likelihood ratio rescued us in one dimension; there is no ordering to rescue us here.

Demo: drag nu (vermillion) along the boundary theta1 = theta2 from the centre to the edge, then try the other piece. The dashed g = 1 contour stays a straight line through nu, and part of the hatched null is always red (above 1): the max over the null never drops below 1. Then raise n: the region above 1 gets more extreme but the line does not move. Compare widget 1: in 1D the line is a point, and the null fits on one side.

The resolution: mixtures over the null

  • Denominator \(p_W = \int p_{\boldsymbol\theta}\, dW(\boldsymbol\theta)\) for a mixing measure \(W\) on \(\Theta_0\).
  • The reverse information projection \(P^*\) minimises \(\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P_W)\) over such mixtures (Grünwald et al. 2020; Lardy et al. 2024).
  • \(E^* = dQ/dP^*\) is the log-optimal e-variable (Grünwald et al. 2020; Larsson et al. 2025).

Timing: 1 minute. This is where the Bayesian and the martingale stories meet: E* is a Bayes factor whose null prior is the projection.

Adding an atom moves the region above 1

Timing: 2 minutes. With two atoms the g = 1 contour is no longer a line, and it can bend around the corner of the null.

Scroll over an atom to change its weight, drag to move it, double-click to remove it. “Frank-Wolfe step” adds an atom where g_W is largest over the null: the oracle. “Reweight” is one EM weight update with the atoms fixed; it cannot increase KL.

How do you find the RIPr?

  • Minimising \(\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P_W)\) over \(W\) is fitting a mixture model to \(Q\), with atoms constrained to \(\Theta_0\).
  • Convex in the weights, not in the atoms. The null is not convex either.
  • Three algorithms:
    • Frank–Wolfe (Frank and Wolfe 1956; Jaggi 2013; Tuynman and Mathieu 2026) and Li–Barron (Li and Barron 1999): add one atom at a time.
    • Expectation–maximisation: move and reweight a fixed set of atoms. TODO: add Dempster, Laird and Rubin (1977) to bibliography.bib from the manuscript.
    • A hybrid: Frank–Wolfe steps interleaved with EM sweeps.

Timing: 1 minute. The oracle, which picks where the next atom goes, is a global non-convex search. That is where the difficulty lives.

The fitting algorithms at work

Timing: 2 minutes. Frank-Wolfe attacks the bound directly but converges slowly. EM is fast but cannot add atoms. The hybrid takes both.

Close is not enough

  • If \(g_W > 1\) anywhere in the null, \(dQ/dP_W\) is not an e-variable at all.
  • An approximate projection, however close, gives an unfair bet (Grünwald et al. 2020).
  • Fix: divide by the worst case, \(G_W = \sup_{\boldsymbol\theta \in \Theta_0} g_W(\boldsymbol\theta)\). \[E_W = \frac{1}{G_W}\frac{dQ}{dP_W} \quad\text{is an e-variable, and}\quad \mathrm{KL}(Q\,\|\,P_W) - \log G_W \le \mathbb{E}_Q[\log E^*] \le \mathrm{KL}(Q\,\|\,P_W).\]

Timing: 1 minute. This is the central idea of the manuscript; slow down here. Careful wording: exceeding 1 somewhere means invalid, not merely suboptimal.

Paying for validity

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Validity is decoupled from the optimiser: any fit, stopped anywhere, gives a real e-variable, and log G_W measures what it cost.

Bounding the worst case

  • A search over \(\Theta_0\) only finds points, so it can only under-report \(G_W\).
  • An upper bound must cover every point: branch and bound over the null.
  • For multinomials, \(g_W\) is already a Bernstein polynomial whose coefficients are the ratios \(r(\boldsymbol c)\). TODO: cite Garloff (1985) and Leroy (2012); add both to bibliography.bib from the manuscript.
  • Implemented in the R package ripr.

bernstein-1d: g_W with its Bernstein control polygon, before and after one subdivision. Spec in the comment above.

Timing: 1 minute. The only rigorous step is the bound, and it is needed once, at the end; cheap searches are enough inside the fitting loop.

A prior over the alternative

  • Replace the point \(\boldsymbol\mu\) by a mixture \(Q = P_{W_1}\).
  • The rescaled \(E_W\) is then a Bayes factor with numerator prior \(W_1\), divided by \(G_W\).
  • This closes the loop with the Bayesian audit: the denominator prior is chosen, not guessed.

Why sampling with replacement?

  • A mathematical device, not a physical requirement: reuse ballots already drawn whenever the model asks for a repeat.
  • The cost is the finite-population correction, expected to be small for large elections.

Is any Bayes factor an e-process for \(K > 2\)?

  • For \(K = 2\) the projection is the tie at every \(n\), so the Bayes factor is a test martingale.
  • For \(K > 2\) preliminary calculations show the projection depends on \(n\).
  • Conjecture: no Bayes factor of this form is an e-process. Not yet proved.
